Reading SPSS output on paper

Everything from sessions 1, 2, 4 and 5 on one page, with the SPSS output tables you will have to read. Each table is built like the real Output Viewer, with real numbers from the chocolate dataset, red markers on the cells to read, and the exact sentence to write.

MCQ · 20%Theory and application: measurement levels, which test, hypotheses, reading a value in a table.
Open questions · 30%Output interpretation and reporting: read the right cells, write the full sentence with symbols and decimals.
Open book (printed)Print this page and the course slides. Knowing where to look in each table is what saves time.
The 5-step routine for every output question

1. What is the DV level and how many IV categories? → test. 2. Which respondents (whole dataset or a subset)? 3. Check the condition (expected counts, Levene). 4. Read the significance (two-sided, ≤ .050?). 5. Only if significant: direction (means / %) and effect size, then the sentence, the hypothesis decision and a managerial conclusion.

About the numbers in the tables

They were calculated from the project dataset with the same formulas as SPSS 29, laid out like the SPSS tables. Older SPSS versions show “.000” where SPSS 29 can show “<.001”; this page shows .000 like the course slides. A few rows SPSS adds that the course never uses are left out and marked as such.

SESSION 1 · 1.1Data View and Variable View

Data View (like Excel)

  • Row = one case (a consumer, a business, a brand): all its answers.
  • Column = one variable (a question).
  • Data from different cases → different rows; different variables → different columns.

Variable View (the dictionary)

  • Row = one variable, with its characteristics.
  • Name, Type, Label, Values, Missing, Measure.
  • GDPR: always anonymise (no names, e-mails, IP, ID numbers, health or political data…).

SESSION 1 · 1.2Measurement levels

LevelFamilyPropertiesExamples
NominalCategorical2+ categories, description only, no orderGender, colour, yes/no, instruments played, phone numbers
OrdinalCategoricalCategories with a logical order, distances unknownEducation level, chart position, ranking of brands, scale with fewer than 7 points
IntervalMetric (Scale)Equal distances (+ and − make sense), no true zeroScale of 7 points or more, temperature in °C, year
RatioMetric (Scale)Equal distances and a true, meaningful zero (× and ÷ make sense)Browsing time, money earned, number of downloads, age
Traps from the session 1 quiz

Chart position = ordinal (we know the order, not the gap). Year = interval (equal distances, but year 0 is not “no year”). Money and time = ratio. Rank-ordering 30 children from 30 to 1 turns IQ into an ordinal variable. A 1–5 rating is ordinal, a 1–7 rating is interval.

SESSION 1 · 1.3Independent and dependent variable

IV · independent · predictorThe supposed cause. Its value does not depend on another variable. Manipulated variables are always IVs.
DV · dependent · outcomeThe effect: what changes because of the IV.

Examples from the slides: white vs red wine (IV) → life expectancy (DV). Classical vs hip-hop music (IV) → time spent in store (DV). Ad A/B/C/D seen (IV, nominal) → perceived relevance 1–5 (ordinal) and purchase intention 1–10 (interval).

SESSION 1 · 1.5 + S4Sample, population and confidence interval

Your dataset is a sample; you want to know the population. Samples vary because they contain different people (sampling variation), so the sample mean is not exactly the population mean. The 95% confidence interval is the range that contains the population mean in 95% of samples: “we are 95% confident the population mean lies within [lower; upper]”.

You find CIs in SPSS in the Descriptives of ANOVA, in the t-test tables (“95% Confidence Interval of the Difference”) and as error bars on bar charts.

SESSION 2 · 2.4Summated scales and Cronbach's α

Why: complex constructs (attitude, experience, quality) need several items, and analysing item per item is confusing. You summate items only if all 4 requirements are met; otherwise you analyse items separately.

#RequirementHow you check it
1Same answering formatSurvey (e.g. all 1–7 agree/disagree)
2Asked togetherSurvey (one matrix question after one overall question)
3Same underlying construct (or sub-concepts)Common sense on the meaning (e.g. “pleasure” vs “planning” in shopping)
4Internally reliableCronbach's α after recoding reversed items; 2 items → Pearson r

Warnings (S2)

Items must match logically (garbage in, garbage out) and be phrased in the same direction: recode by mirroring (1↔7, 2↔6, 3↔5, 4 stays) into a different variable. Min 3 items, max 10 (α rises with the number of items). If α changes only slightly, keep more items: remove an item only if α would move to another level.

The S2 checklist for data preparation (8 points)
  1. Type (order: recode into different variable, then string → numeric), measure, name, label.
  2. All value labels for categorical variables; end-points for invented metric ones.
  3. Impossible values: missing or fixed, and reported.
  4. Items that could be summated: check all requirements.
  5. New variables / scales (Compute): report the calculation.
  6. Metric → categorical (Recode into Different Variables): how many categories? report.
  7. Repeat 1–3 for each new variable.
  8. Cases to exclude for an RQ (Select Cases): report, and turn off afterwards.

SESSION 4 · 4.1Hypotheses and the p-value

H1 · alternative = your predictionCause → effect (IV → DV) · specific DV (not “good”) · benchmark in the IV (“than…”) · clear direction (more / less) · testable.
“People who are hungry will buy more products than people who are not hungry.”
H0 · null = no effectSame structure, but equal / the same / similar (never “not bigger”, which could mean smaller).
“People who are hungry will buy the same amount of products as people who are not hungry.”

Null hypothesis significance testing

  • We assume H0 is true in the population. The p-value = the probability of finding this effect in the dataset if H0 were true, i.e. the probability the effect is due to chance.
  • p ≤ .050 → significant → reject H0; H1 is supported only if the effect goes in the predicted direction.
  • p > .050 → not significant → cannot reject H0: no effect in the population. Report the means, but never say one is higher.
  • We are only interested in the population; the dataset only gives us the data.
H1 predictedResultConclusion
Thinking about chocolate → eat morep = .020, they eat moreH0 rejected, H1 supported
Thinking about chocolate → eat morep = .020, they eat lessH0 rejected, but H1 not supported (opposite direction)
Thinking about chocolate → eat morep = .350H0 not rejected: same amount

Statistical significance vs effect size

Significance says if the effect is real in the population. The effect size (Phi / Cramer's V, Cohen's d, eta²) says if it is big enough to matter for the company. Look at it only if the test is significant.

SESSION 5 · 5.3One-way ANOVA

Metric DV, more than 2 independent categories. H0: all means are equal; H1: at least one differs (state which one, e.g. “high > medium & low”). The ANOVA says whether there is a difference; the post hoc tests say which pairs differ.

Analyze › Compare Means and Proportions › One-Way ANOVA → DV in Dependent List, IV in Factor → Estimate effect size → Post Hoc: Bonferroni and Tamhane's T2 → Options: Descriptive, Homogeneity of variance test, Welch. Example: does chocolate frequency differ between the 3 health segments?

C3Self-test: 25 exam-style questions

Answer first, then open the card.

The number of downloads of a song: which measurement level?

Ratio: equal distances and a true zero (no downloads).

The position of a band in the download chart?

Ordinal: we know the order, not how many more downloads.

A satisfaction question on a 1–5 scale: interval or ordinal for this course?

Ordinal (fewer than 7 points). You cannot run a t-test on it.

Which value labels does a 1–7 agree scale need? And age?

Agree scale (invented metric): the end-points (1 and 7). Age (natural scale): none.

A string variable contains “yes”/“no”. In which order do you fix it?

First replace the words by numbers (Data View or Recode into Different Variables), then change the Type to Numeric, then add value labels.

Which central tendency measures can you use for an ordinal variable?

Mode and median (not the mean).

Report: N = 10, mode 1 and 2, median 2, mean 2.6, SD 1.71.

Mo = 1.00–2.00, Md = 2.00, M = 2.60, SD = 1.71.

A 1–7 item contains the value 12. What do you do?

Impossible value. Not obvious what it should be → declare 12 as missing in Variable View, and report it.

Customer length = 1173 days in a dataset where everyone is between 100 and 300.

Obvious typing mistake with one solution → fix it by hand in Data View (173), and report it.

Cumulative percents: 30.2, 35.1, … 64.0, 69.5. Where do you cut for 3 groups?

After 35.1 (|35.1 − 33.33| = 1.8 < 3.1) and after 64.0 (|64.0 − 66.66| = 2.7 < 2.8).

α = .67 with 4 items; deleting item 3 gives α = .74. Keep or remove item 3?

Remove it from the scale: α moves from acceptable to good (another level). Keep it in the dataset.

α = .91; deleting an item gives .93. Keep or remove?

Keep: both are excellent. With a small difference, keep more items.

You only have 2 items for a construct. How do you check reliability?

Pearson correlation (Analyze › Correlate › Bivariate), same thresholds as α, reported as r = .xx.

Write H0 and H1: “Does mood affect product attitude?”

H1: People in a positive mood will have a more positive product attitude than people in a negative mood. H0: People in a positive mood will have a similar product attitude to people in a negative mood.

p = .032 but the difference goes the opposite way from H1. Conclusion?

Significant, H0 rejected, but H1 not supported (opposite direction).

2×2 crosstab, one expected count = 4.2. Can you use the Chi²?

No: in a 2×2 table no expected count may be below 5. Merge or stop.

3×4 crosstab, 2 of 12 cells below 5, minimum expected 1.9. OK?

Yes: 16.7% ≤ 20% and all expected counts > 1.

Chi² significant, Cramer's V = .23. How big is the effect?

Small (.20–.29).

One-sample t-test with N = 240: what is the df?

df = N − 1 = 239.

Each customer rated both brands out of 10. Which test?

Paired (dependent) samples t-test: same people in both IV categories.

Levene Sig. = .210 in an independent t-test. Which row?

Top row (equal variances assumed), because .210 > .050.

Cohen's d = −.85. Interpretation?

Big difference (|d| ≥ .80); the minus sign gives the direction (group 1 or the sample is lower).

ANOVA, Levene p = .003. Which tables do you read?

Robust Tests (Welch) and the Tamhane block of Multiple Comparisons.

Welch's F(2, 197.58) = 1.20, p = .304. Do you look at the post hoc tests?

No. The overall test is not significant: all means are equal in the population.

eta² = .05. Size of the effect?

Medium (.04–.14).
