02Writing rules from the course

Do

2 decimals for M, SE, SD, t, F, χ², d, η², V, α, r.

3 decimals for p; write p < .001 when SPSS shows .000.

No 0 before values that cannot exceed 1: .05, α = .77, r = .72. Keep it for 10.50 or 34.74.

df in brackets, without decimals; Welch and “equal variances not assumed” df get 2 decimals.

Report M and SE for every group, even when the result is not significant.

Effect size only when the test is significant, with its label (small, moderate, big).

End with the hypothesis conclusion and a managerial conclusion.

Don't

Say a group is higher when the test is not significant. Write that they are equal in the population.

Paste SPSS tables or screenshots of output in the text (appendix only).

Write p = .000 or p = 0.005.

Report only the part that answers the hypothesis.

Explain menus and clicks (“we clicked Analyze…”). Name the test and the variables instead.

Use M and SD for test results: the course asks for M and SE.

Symbols in Word

Put statistical symbols in italics: M, SE, t, F, p, d, r. Insert χ² and η² with Insert › Symbol (Greek), or type “X²” and “eta²” as the slides allow.

A1 · ReportExecutive summary (½ page)

The instructions say a manager will not read 50 pages. The executive summary gives them the answer in 30 seconds. Write it last.

Executive summary

A chocolate manufacturer wants to know which consumers to target with its new healthy chocolate. Using a survey of 691 Belgian consumers, we split the market into three segments based on health concern: high (34%), medium (29%) and low (37%).

The high-concern segment is an attractive target. These consumers eat chocolate more often than the other two segments and find it more attractive than the low-concern segment. For them, chocolate is more frequent and more attractive than candy, salty snacks or cookies. They are younger than average (31 vs 35 years), more often women, students and people living with their parents. They eat chocolate for pure indulgence and when they feel lonely, mostly after dinner and late at night, and they buy it more often in night shops, vending machines and petrol stations. Within this segment, women have less self-control than men.

A positioning as “the ideal snack to kill hunger between meals” is not recommended. Only 16% of consumers eat chocolate for that reason, and they find it only slightly more attractive than others.

We recommend targeting young, health-concerned consumers, especially women, with a “guilt-free indulgence” positioning, and distributing the product in impulse channels as well as supermarkets.

A3 · Requirement 2Data preparation section (example text)

The instructions say to “report how you handled outliers, impossible values, provide detailed information about new variables you created, Cronbach’s alpha or Pearson Correlation”. Session 2 says to be transparent: every change needs to be reported, with old and new values.

3.1 Names, labels, values and measures

Example

The original dataset had 691 respondents and 73 variables without labels, all set to Nominal. We renamed the socio-demographic variables with meaningless codes (q42 → gender, q4301 → age, q44 → living, q46 → education, q47 → occupation, q48 → native_lang, q49 → province, q50 → area). We gave every variable a label with the survey question and added value labels. Finally we set the measurement level of each variable (see Figure 1).

3.2 Impossible values

Example

We checked the frequency tables of all variables for impossible values. We found one answer of 8 in the frequency of eating salty snacks and one answer of 9 in the fit of chocolate after work/school, both on a 1–7 scale. Neither is an obvious mistake with only one possible correction, so we indicated both as missing. Age ranges from 11 to 77 years. Young respondents are possible, so we kept all ages; 24 respondents did not give their age. No other variable contained values outside its scale.

“Outliers” in the instructions

The project slides use the word “outliers”. The course (Session 2) treats them as impossible values: outside the scale range, extremely unlikely, or unusable. Say this explicitly, so the grader sees you applied the course definition.

3.3 Household size (text → number)

Example

Household size (Q20) was recorded as text. We recoded it into a new numeric variable, hhsize. Written answers were translated into the number they describe (e.g. “four” → 4, “2 (me and partner)” → 2). Answers without a single interpretation (“4/5”, “/”) and impossible answers (0 people, 19 respondents) were set to missing. hhsize has 617 valid answers (1 to 9 people) and 74 missing.

3.4 Recoded items and summated scales

Example

Three questions contain several items that could be combined: feelings while eating chocolate (Q2), self-control (Q14) and feelings after eating chocolate (Q16). For each one we checked the four requirements: same answer format (1–7), asked together in one matrix, same underlying construct, and internal reliability (Cronbach’s α).

Based on meaning, the 17 feelings contain two sub-concepts: positive moods (9 items) and negative moods (8 items). The health-concern items “very satisfied/happy” and “full of joy” are phrased in the opposite direction from “worried”, “guilty” and “ashamed”. We therefore recoded them by mirroring (1 = almost always – 7 = never instead of 1 = never – 7 = almost always).

All four scales are reliable (Table 1). For positive feelings, deleting “Cool” would raise α from .93 to .97. Both values are excellent, so following the course rule we kept all items. We computed each scale as the average of its items, using the recoded items for health concern. Summated scales are interval variables.

3.5 The three health-concern segments

Example

To identify the three segments, we split the health-concern scale into three groups of about one third each. In the frequency table we cut after the cumulative percentages closest to 33.33% and 66.66%. This gives low concern (up to 2.80 → 3, n = 252, 36.5%), medium concern (3.00 to 3.80 → 2, n = 202, 29.2%) and high concern (4.00 and more → 1, n = 237, 34.3%). The new variable seg is ordinal.

3.6 Other preparation (only if you did it)

Example

For the Chi² tests in RQ1, the expected-count condition was not met for occupation, education and native language. We therefore merged categories into new variables: occupation3 (1 = student; 2–6 = working; 7–10 = not working/other), education3 (1–4 = secondary or less; 5–6 = bachelor or non-university master; 7–8 = university master or PhD; 9 “other”, n = 3, set to missing) and native_lang3 (Dutch; French; bilingual or other). For RQ3 and RQ5 we selected only the high-concern segment (seg = 1) and switched the selection off afterwards.

RQ1 · ExampleProfile of the high-concern segment

4.1 Research question. How do consumers with high health concern differ from the medium and low segments in socio-demographics, in when and why they eat chocolate, and in where they buy it? The answer tells the manager who the target is and how to reach them.

4.2 Method. We used all respondents. The IV is the health-concern segment (3 independent categories). For the categorical DVs (gender, survey language, living situation, education, occupation, native language, province, living area and the nine motives) we used Chi² tests of independence. For the metric DVs (age, household size, the 13 time spots and occasions, and the 7 places to buy) we used one-way ANOVAs.

4.3 Hypotheses (only if RQ1 is one of your two presented RQs)
H0: Consumers with high health concern have the same profile (socio-demographics, moments, motives and places of purchase) as consumers with medium and low health concern.
H1: Consumers with high health concern have a different profile from consumers with medium and low health concern.

4.4 Results — socio-demographics and motives (Chi²). For all tests the expected-count condition was met, after merging categories for occupation, education and native language (see 3.6). Table 2 summarises the results.

The chi-square test between segment and gender is significant with a negligible effect (χ²(2) = 9.15, p = .010, Cramer’s V = .12). Comparing the column percentages horizontally, the share of women rises with health concern: 59.1% in the high segment, 54.5% in the medium and 45.6% in the low segment.

The chi-square test between segment and occupation is highly significant with a negligible effect (χ²(4) = 26.03, p < .001, Cramer’s V = .14). Students make up 31.6% of the high segment, against 26.2% of the medium and 14.3% of the low segment.

The chi-square test between segment and education is not significant (χ²(4) = 2.12, p = .713): the three segments have the same education level in the population.

4.4 Results — when and where (ANOVA). Table 3 shows the means per segment.

For “late at night”, Levene’s test showed unequal variances, so we report Welch’s test. An Anova indicated that there was a significant effect of health concern on how well chocolate fits late at night (Welch’s F(2, 435.03) = 22.61, p < .001). There was no significant difference between the medium and low segments (p > .050). The difference is medium (η² = .07).

For “after dinner”, variances were equal. An Anova indicated a significant effect (F(2, 688) = 11.93, p < .001). The difference is small (η² = .03).

There was no significant effect on buying chocolate in a large supermarket (Welch’s F(2, 453.45) = 0.57, p = .566): the three segments buy there equally often.

4.5 Interpretation and managerial implication

Compared with the low-concern segment, highly concerned consumers are more often women, younger, students and people living with their parents. They eat chocolate more for pure indulgence and when they feel lonely, especially after dinner and late at night, and they buy it more in impulse channels (night shops, vending machines, petrol stations, bookshops), while supermarket purchases are the same for all segments. All effects are small, so the segments overlap a lot. For the product manager, this means the healthy chocolate should be sold in supermarkets and in impulse channels, with communication aimed at young people and evening moments of indulgence.

RQ2 · Full model sectionDo they eat more chocolate and find it more attractive?

This is a complete section, written the way a presented RQ should look. The other RQs follow the same pattern.

5. RQ2 — How attractive is the high-concern segment?

5.1 Research question. Do consumers with high health concern eat chocolate more often, and find chocolate more attractive, than consumers with medium and low health concern? If they do, they are a valuable segment to target.

5.2 Method. We used all respondents (N = 691). The IV is the health-concern segment, with 3 independent categories (high, medium, low). The DVs are the frequency of eating chocolate (freq1) and the attractiveness of chocolate (att1), both interval variables on 7-point scales. With a metric DV and more than two independent groups, we used a one-way ANOVA with post hoc tests.

5.3 Hypotheses.
H0a: Highly concerned consumers eat chocolate as often as medium and low concerned consumers (Mhigh = Mmedium = Mlow).
H1a: Highly concerned consumers eat chocolate more often than medium and low concerned consumers (Mhigh > Mmedium & Mlow).
H0b: Highly concerned consumers find chocolate as attractive as medium and low concerned consumers.
H1b: Highly concerned consumers find chocolate more attractive than medium and low concerned consumers.

5.4 Results. Levene’s test showed unequal variances for both frequency (p = .048) and attractiveness (p = .020). We therefore report Welch’s test and Tamhane post hoc tests.

Frequency. An Anova indicated that there was a significant effect of health concern on how often people eat chocolate (Welch’s F(2, 450.91) = 20.25, p < .001). Tamhane post hoc tests showed that highly concerned consumers (M = 4.97, SE = .09) eat chocolate significantly more often than medium (M = 4.55, SE = .10, p = .007) and low concerned consumers (M = 4.12, SE = .10, p < .001). Medium concerned consumers also eat chocolate more often than low concerned consumers (p = .008). The difference is medium (η² = .06). Therefore H1a is supported.

Attractiveness. An Anova indicated that there was a significant effect of health concern on the attractiveness of chocolate (Welch’s F(2, 450.85) = 8.29, p < .001). Tamhane post hoc tests showed that highly concerned consumers (M = 5.84, SE = .09) find chocolate significantly more attractive than low concerned consumers (M = 5.27, SE = .11, p < .001), but equally attractive as medium concerned consumers (M = 5.53, SE = .11, p > .050). Medium and low concerned consumers also do not differ (p > .050). The difference is small (η² = .02). Therefore H1b is partially supported.

5.5 Interpretation and managerial implication. The high-concern segment is the most valuable one. Its members eat chocolate the most often (about a couple of times a week) and like it at least as much as the other segments. Their health concern does not stop them from eating chocolate; it makes them feel guilty about it. A healthy chocolate answers exactly this tension: it lets heavy users keep their habit with less guilt. The manager should target this segment first, as it combines high volume with a clear need.

RQ3 · ExampleChocolate vs other snacks

6.2 Method. We selected only the high-concern segment (seg = 1, n = 237). The IV is the type of snack, with 2 dependent categories each time: the same respondents rated chocolate and the other snack. The DVs are frequency and attractiveness (interval, 1–7). We ran six paired-samples t-tests: chocolate against candy, salty snacks and cookies, for both frequency and attractiveness.

6.4 Results. A dependent sample t-test indicated that highly concerned consumers eat chocolate (M = 4.97, SE = .09) significantly more often than candy (M = 3.59, SE = .10; t(236) = 11.00, p < .001), a moderate difference (d = .71). They also eat chocolate significantly more often than salty snacks (M = 3.64, SE = .08; t(235) = 11.58, p < .001, d = .75) and cookies (M = 4.32, SE = .09; t(236) = 5.77, p < .001, d = .37). In the same way, they find chocolate (M = 5.84, SE = .09) significantly more attractive than candy (M = 4.43, SE = .11; t(236) = 9.90, p < .001, d = .64), salty snacks (M = 4.96, SE = .10; t(236) = 6.02, p < .001, d = .39) and cookies (M = 4.99, SE = .08; t(236) = 7.62, p < .001, d = .49). All differences are moderate (Table 4, Figure 3).

RQ4 · ExampleOlder or younger than average?

7.2 Method. First we computed the mean age of all respondents (M = 34.74, n = 667). Then we selected the high-concern segment (seg = 1) and compared its mean age with 34.74 using a one-sample t-test. Age is a ratio variable, and there is one group compared with a known value.

7.4 Results. One-sample t-test indicated that highly concerned consumers were significantly younger than the average age of 34.74 years in the dataset (M = 31.47, SE = .75; t(231) = −4.36, p < .001). The difference is moderate (d = −.29). Therefore H1 is supported.

7.5 Implication. The target segment is about three years younger than the average consumer, which matches RQ1 (more students, more people living with their parents). Media and tone of voice should fit young adults, for example social media and campus or student-city activations.

Graph

A simple bar with the segment’s mean age next to a reference line at 34.74 (Chart Builder, then add a Y-axis reference line in the Chart Editor), or two bars made in Excel: “High concern 31.47” and “All respondents 34.74”.

RQ5 · ExampleMen vs women in the high segment

8.2 Method. We selected the high-concern segment (seg = 1; 97 men, 140 women). The IV is gender (2 independent categories). The DVs are the summated scales self-control, positive feelings and negative feelings (interval). We used independent-samples t-tests.

8.4 Results. For all three DVs, Levene’s test was not significant (p = .441, .703 and .244), so we read the “equal variances assumed” row.

Independent sample t-test indicated that, among highly concerned consumers, men (M = 4.32, SE = .16) have significantly more self-control than women (M = 3.70, SE = .14; t(235) = 2.84, p = .005). The difference is moderate (d = .37). Women (M = 3.81, SE = .09) eat chocolate significantly more when they feel positive than men (M = 3.52, SE = .11; t(235) = −2.08, p = .039), a moderate difference (d = −.27). For negative feelings, men (M = 3.99, SE = .13) and women (M = 4.21, SE = .09) are equal (t(235) = −1.39, p = .166). H1 is supported for self-control and positive feelings; H0 is supported for negative feelings.

8.5 Implication. Women in the target segment find chocolate harder to resist and link it more with positive moods. Since they are also the majority of the segment (RQ1), the main communication can target women with joyful, “treat yourself without guilt” messages. Negative moods are equally important for men and women, so there is no need for a gender-specific message there.

RQ6 · ExampleThe “kill hunger between meals” target group

9.2 Method. We used all respondents. The IV is the motive “to kill hunger between meals” (mot3: 1 = applicable, the potential target group, n = 113; 0 = not applicable, n = 578), 2 independent categories. The DVs are attractiveness of chocolate (att1) and self-control (interval). A lower self-control score means chocolate is less easy to resist. We used independent-samples t-tests.

9.4 Results. For attractiveness, Levene’s test was significant (p = .002), so we read the “equal variances not assumed” row. For self-control it was not (p = .686), so we read the top row.

Independent sample t-test indicated that people who eat chocolate to kill hunger between meals (M = 5.78, SE = .12) find chocolate significantly more attractive than other people (M = 5.49, SE = .07; t(184.21) = 2.02, p = .045). The difference is small (d = .18). They find chocolate equally easy to resist as other people (M = 4.01, SE = .17 vs M = 4.36, SE = .07; t(689) = −1.93, p = .054). H1 is supported for attractiveness and H0 for self-control.

9.5 Implication. The “kill hunger” positioning reaches only 16% of consumers, who like chocolate only slightly more and resist it just as well as others. It is a weak basis for the brand. It could be a secondary message (e.g. a snack-size format), but not the main positioning.

C1 · Requirement 7Recommendations for the product manager

The instructions ask for recommendations “in the light of more general marketing principles”. Organise them with frameworks a manager knows, such as segmentation–targeting–positioning (STP) and the 4P, and link every point to the RQ that supports it. That shows the recommendation comes from your data and not from opinion.

10. Recommendations

Targeting. Target the high-concern segment (34% of the market). It eats chocolate the most often (RQ2) and prefers chocolate to every other snack (RQ3), so it combines high volume with a clear need for a healthier option. Within it, give priority to young adults (RQ4: 31 years on average), students and people living with their parents (RQ1), and women, who are the majority of the segment and have less self-control (RQ1, RQ5).

Positioning. Position the product as “guilt-free indulgence”: the same pleasure as regular chocolate, without the guilt. This matches why the segment eats chocolate (pure indulgence, RQ1) and how they feel afterwards (worried, guilty, the basis of the segment). Do not use “the ideal snack to kill hunger between meals” as the main positioning: it concerns only 16% of consumers and the effect is small (RQ6).

Product. Since the target compares the product with regular chocolate (RQ3), taste parity must be the core claim. Small portions or single bars fit the evening and late-night moments (RQ1) and help women who find chocolate hard to resist control the amount (RQ5).

Place. Keep a strong presence in supermarkets, where all segments buy equally (RQ1), and add impulse channels where the high-concern segment buys more: night shops, vending machines (e.g. on campuses), petrol stations and bookshops (RQ1).

Promotion. Use channels that reach young adults (social media, student events). Use joyful, positive messages aimed at women (RQ5) and moments after dinner and late at night (RQ1). Comforting messages around being alone can address the “when lonely” motive (RQ1).

Price. The data do not include price. In earlier attempts, healthy variants failed partly because they were too expensive (case background), so price should stay close to regular chocolate. A test of willingness to pay is advised.

Limitations. Most differences between segments are small (η² ≤ .09, Cramer’s V ≤ .15), so the segments overlap. The results are based on self-reported behaviour in Belgium.
