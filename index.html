IÉSEG · Data Insights · SPSS group project

Healthy chocolate: who should we sell it to?

A click-by-click walkthrough of the whole project: cleaning the 691-respondent dataset, building the scales and the three health concern segments, then running and reporting the right test for each of the six research questions. Each step says what to click and why you are doing it.

The story you are telling

A chocolate maker added a strong antioxidant to its chocolate, so it is now tasty and healthier. The natural target is people who are very concerned about their health. Your job: split the market into high / medium / low health concern and describe the high segment.

PART A · STEP A2Impossible values, missing values and outliers

Session 2 material. First find the problems, then tell SPSS to ignore them.

1

Find impossible values with Frequencies

An impossible value is an answer that cannot exist, like an 8 on a 1–7 scale (a typing error during data entry). If you leave it, it pulls the mean up and distorts every test that uses that variable. The fastest way to spot it: ask SPSS the minimum and maximum of every variable.

  1. Analyze›Descriptive Statistics›Frequencies…
  2. In the left list click feel1, scroll down, Shift-click g5 → press the arrow ➜. All the survey items move to Variable(s).
  3. Click Statistics… → under “Dispersion” tick Minimum and Maximum → Continue.
  4. Untick Display frequency tables at the bottom-left (otherwise you get 60 tables). SPSS may warn that you asked for no output: click OK.
  5. Click OK. The Output Viewer opens with one wide Statistics table.

Read the output

PART A · STEP A3Summated scales with Cronbach's α

Session 3 material. You need three scales: feelings (Q2), self-control (Q14) and health concern (Q16).

A survey asks several questions about the same idea (e.g. “worried”, “guilty”, “ashamed” all measure health concern). Instead of running every test 3 times, you average the items into one score. But you are only allowed to do that if the items really measure the same thing: that is what Cronbach's α checks. A high α means people who score high on one item also score high on the others.

Decision rules

α ≥ .70 = reliable (≥ .60 acceptable in exploratory research). An item fits if its Corrected Item-Total Correlation ≥ .30. If “Cronbach's Alpha if Item Deleted” is clearly higher than the current α, the item hurts the scale: remove it and run again. All items must point in the same direction (reverse-code if needed). For a 2-item scale use the Pearson correlation instead of α.

1

How to run a reliability analysis

  1. Analyze›Scale›Reliability Analysis…
  2. Move the items of one scale into Items (Ctrl/⌘-click to pick non-adjacent ones). Model: Alpha. Scale label: e.g. “Health concern”.
  3. Statistics… → under “Descriptives for” tick Item and Scale if item deleted; under “Inter-Item” tick Correlations → Continue → OK.

Read the output

Reliability Statistics
Cronbach's Alpha and N of Items. This is the number you report.
Inter-Item Correlation Matrix
Correlations between each pair of items. A negative correlation means an item is reversed. A value near 0 means the item measures something else.
Item-Total Statistics
Two key columns: Corrected Item-Total Correlation (should be ≥ .30) and Cronbach's Alpha if Item Deleted (if higher than the α above, drop that item).
2

Feelings scale: split it in two, drop “Cool”

Eating chocolate when happy and eating chocolate when depressed are two different behaviours, so logically the 17 feelings form two concepts: positive moods and negative moods. Test each group separately.

  1. Run Reliability with feel1, feel2, feel5, feel6, feel8, feel9, feel10, feel14, feel16 (positive).
  2. In Item-Total Statistics, feel10 “Cool” has an item-total correlation of .03 and α if deleted .965 (> .925). → Run again without feel10.
  3. Run Reliability with feel3, feel4, feel7, feel11, feel12, feel13, feel15, feel17 (negative).

3

Self-control scale

RQ5 and RQ6 ask about self-control (“how easy is it to resist chocolate”). Three questions measure it, so you combine them into one score.

Run Reliability with f0701, f0702, f0703. All three point the same way: higher = more self-control = easier to resist.

α = .917. Item-total r between .79 and .86. Deleting f0701 gives .920, a negligible gain, and the item clearly measures the same idea, so keep all three.

4

Health concern scale: the trap with g4 and g5

This is the most important scale: it decides who is in the target segment. g1–g3 (worried, guilty, ashamed) are negative feelings after eating = concern. g4–g5 (satisfied, full of joy) are positive: they go the opposite way.

  2. Option 1: reverse g4 and g5 (see below) and test all five. Option 2: keep only g1, g2, g3. Compare the α of both.

PART A · STEP A4Cut health concern into 3 segments

health is a continuous score (1.00 to 7.00). The research questions talk about segments (high / medium / low), which is a category. So you cut the score in three groups of roughly equal size (tertiles). This new variable seg is the IV of RQ1–RQ2 and the filter of RQ3–RQ5.

1

Visual Binning

Before each RQ, answer 4 questions (instructions, step 1–3): Which respondents? What is the IV and its level? What is the DV and its level? So which test? Every RQ below starts with a box that answers them.

S

Analyse only one segment

RQ3, RQ4 and RQ5 are about the high-concern people only. A filter hides the others from every analysis until you switch it off.

  1. Data›Select Cases…
  2. Choose If condition is satisfied → If… → move seg to the box and type seg = 1 → Continue.

Reading the p-value

Every test gives a Sig. (= p-value): the probability of seeing a difference this big if H0 were true. p ≤ .05 → reject H0 (the difference is significant). p > .05 → you cannot reject H0 (no proof of a difference). SPSS writes .000 when p < .001.

Reading Levene's test (t-test and ANOVA)

The classic t-test and ANOVA assume the groups have similar spread (variance). Levene checks that. If the assumption fails, SPSS gives you a corrected version of the test, and you must read that one instead.

  • Levene p > .05 → equal variances assumed → t-test: read the top row; ANOVA: read the normal F and use Tukey post hoc.
  • Levene p ≤ .05 → equal variances not assumed → t-test: read the bottom row; ANOVA: report the Welch test and use Games-Howell post hoc.

Ideas for recommendations

  • Targeting: concerned consumers are heavy, enthusiastic chocolate eaters who feel guilty: a real opportunity. Focus on women and younger adults (students over-represented).
  • Positioning: “indulge without guilt”. They eat for pleasure and comfort (indulgence, loneliness), so an emotional message fits better than a functional “kills hunger” message.
  • Distribution: be present in impulse channels (vending machines, night shops, petrol stations, bookshops) where this segment buys more, besides supermarkets.
  • Moments: mid-morning, at work, after dinner and late at night: snack formats and single portions.
