A2 · ModelRQ slides: question, hypotheses, method

4
RQ1
Who is the health-concerned consumer, compared with the others?
Hypotheses

H0 Highly concerned consumers have the same socio-demographic profile, eat chocolate at the same moments and for the same reasons, and buy it in the same places as medium and low concerned consumers.

H1 Highly concerned consumers differ from medium and low concerned consumers in their socio-demographic profile, when and why they eat chocolate, and where they buy it.

Method

Respondents All 691

IV Health-concern segment (high / medium / low)

DV Gender, age, occupation, living situation… · motives · moments · places to buy

Tests Chi² + Cramer’s V (categorical DVs) · One-way ANOVA + post hoc (scores and age)

8
RQ6
Is “the ideal snack to kill hunger between meals” a good positioning?
Hypotheses

H0 People who eat chocolate to kill hunger between meals find chocolate as attractive and as easy to resist as other people.

H1 People who eat chocolate to kill hunger between meals find chocolate more attractive and less easy to resist than other people.

Method

Respondents All 691

IV Eats chocolate “to kill hunger between meals”: yes (113) / no (578)

DV Attractiveness of chocolate (1–7) · self-control score (1–7)

Test Independent-samples t-test (×2) + Cohen’s d

Good hypothesis (S4)

Cause (segment or motive) → effect, a specific DV, a benchmark (“than other people”), a direction (“more attractive, less easy to resist”), testable. H0 says “same / as … as”.

Avoid

“H0: they don’t find it more attractive” (that could mean equal or less). Saying “self-control” without explaining that a low score = harder to resist.

A4 · Model“What it means” slides: conclusion + recommendation

7
RQ1 · What it means
Our target: young women and students who indulge in the evening
Hypotheses

H1 partially supported They differ on gender, age, occupation, living situation, 2 motives, most moments and impulse places. They are the same on education, language, region, household size and supermarkets.

In our words

The health-concerned consumer is younger, more often a woman or a student, eats chocolate as a treat or when lonely, in the evening, and often buys it on impulse. The differences are real but small: the segments overlap.

For the product manager

→ Targeting: young adults, women, students.

→ Place: keep supermarkets (same for all) and add impulse channels: night shops, vending machines on campuses, petrol stations.

→ Promotion: evening and “me-time” moments, social media.

10
RQ6 · What it means
“Kill hunger between meals” is not a strong positioning
Hypotheses

H1 supported Attractiveness: they find chocolate more attractive (small difference).

H0 supported Self-control: they resist chocolate as easily as other people.

→ H1 partially supported.

In our words

This group is small (16%), likes chocolate only a little more, and is not more “addicted”. It is also not more common among health-concerned consumers (RQ1).

For the product manager

→ Don’t make “kill hunger” the main positioning: the market is small and the effect is weak.

→ Position as guilt-free indulgence, which fits the target’s motives (RQ1).

→ “Between meals” can be a secondary message, e.g. a snack-size bar.

Link to marketing principles

The instructions ask for recommendations “in the light of more general marketing principles”. RQ1 is segmentation and targeting (who) plus place and promotion (where, when). RQ6 is positioning (which promise) and market size: a segment must be big enough and responsive to be worth targeting.

RQ2 · Do they eat more and like it more?

The full model is in A2–A4 above. Key numbers: frequency 4.97 / 4.55 / 4.12, all pairs different, η² = .06 (medium); attractiveness 5.84 / 5.53 / 5.27, high > low only, η² = .02 (small).

RQ3 · Chocolate vs other snacks (high segment)

Respondents
Only the 237 highly concerned
IV
Type of snack (same people rate each)
DV
Frequency (1–7), attractiveness (1–7)
Test
6 paired-samples t-tests + Cohen’s d
H0Highly concerned consumers eat chocolate as often as candy, salty snacks and cookies, and find it equally attractive.
H1Highly concerned consumers eat chocolate more often than candy, salty snacks and cookies, and find it more attractive.

Graph: 2 bar charts (frequency, attractiveness) with 4 bars each: chocolate highlighted. Frequency: chocolate 4.97 · cookies 4.32 · salty 3.64 · candy 3.59. Attractiveness: chocolate 5.84 · cookies 4.99 · salty 4.96 · candy 4.43.

Key numbers: all 6 differences significant (p < .001), moderate effects (d from .37 to .75). H1 supported.

Recommendation: chocolate is their favourite snack, so the competitor is their usual chocolate. Communicate “same taste as your favourite chocolate, less guilt”. Shelf placement next to regular chocolate.

RQ4 · Older or younger than average?

Respondents
Only the highly concerned (232 with an age)
IV
1 group, compared with the average of all (34.74 years)
DV
Age
Test
One-sample t-test + Cohen’s d
H0Highly concerned consumers have the same age as the average consumer (34.74 years).
H1Highly concerned consumers are younger than the average consumer.

Choose the direction of H1 before looking at the results, based on reasoning (e.g. young people are more image- and health-conscious). If you choose “older”, the result is significant in the other direction and H1 is not supported.

Recommendation: young-adult targeting: social media, student cities, tone of voice for 25–35-year-olds.

RQ5 · Men vs women (high segment)

Respondents
Only the 237 highly concerned (97 men, 140 women)
IV
Gender (2 independent groups)
DV
Self-control · positive feelings · negative feelings (scales, 1–7)
Test
3 independent-samples t-tests + Cohen’s d
H0Among highly concerned consumers, men and women have the same self-control and eat chocolate equally when they feel positive or negative.
H1Among highly concerned consumers, women have less self-control than men and eat chocolate more when they feel positive or negative.

Key numbers: self-control significant (p = .005, moderate, d = .37); positive feelings significant (p = .039, moderate, d = −.27); negative feelings no difference (p = .166). H1 partially supported.

Recommendation: women resist less and eat for happy moods → joyful “treat yourself without guilt” messages aimed at women; small portions to help control.

On slide 3, explain the 3 scales in one line each (e.g. “Positive feelings = how often you eat chocolate when happy, joyful, relaxed…”).

B2Statistics → manager language

The instructions say: tell the story “in human language”. Keep the number small on the slide and say the sentence out loud.

StatisticOn the slideWhat you say
p ≤ .050“significant (p = .005)”“This difference is real: it also exists in the whole market, not just in our sample.”
p > .050“no difference (p = .166)”“Men and women are the same here. The small gap in our sample is due to chance.” Never say one is higher.
Mean on 1–7“4.97 / 7”Translate the scale: “about a couple of times a week”, “rather attractive to attractive”.
% (crosstab)“59% women vs 46%”“Out of 10 highly concerned consumers, 6 are women.”
η², d, Cramer’s V“medium effect”, “small effect”“The difference is clear but moderate: the groups still overlap.” A small effect is worth saying, because it tempers the recommendation.
Post hoc tests“High > Medium > Low”“Each step up in health concern means more chocolate.”
Levene / WelchOnly in the method box (“ANOVA (Welch)”)Nothing, unless someone asks: then “the groups were not equally spread, so we used the corrected version of the test”.
Cronbach’s αSlide 3 only: “reliable (α = .77)”“The 5 questions measure the same thing, so we can average them.”

Make the graph readable

Value labels on the bars (4.97); the axis goes from 1 to 7 (or 0), not zoomed in to exaggerate; short labels (“High”, not “seg = 1”); the target group in colour and the others grey; font at least 18 pt.

Avoid

Raw SPSS output tables on a slide; 3D or pie charts; graphs without a title or unit; showing only the significant DV and hiding the others.

C1Design rules

  • One message per slide, written as the title (a full short sentence).
  • Maximum ~30 words of text per slide (except the RQ slide with H0/H1); text at least 18 pt.
  • Same layout for both RQs: question → results → meaning.
  • One colour for the target segment on every slide (e.g. chocolate brown), grey for the others.
  • Slide numbers, so the professor can say “go back to slide 5”.
  • Use the variable meaning, not SPSS names: “how often you eat chocolate”, not freq1.
  • Export to PDF as well as PPTX before sending by email: fonts and layout won’t move.

C2Questions the professor may ask

QuestionPrepared answer
Why this test?Name the DV level and the number of IV groups, and follow the S5 decision tree (e.g. “metric DV, 3 independent groups → ANOVA”).
Why Welch / Tamhane and not the normal ANOVA?“Levene’s test was significant (p ≤ .050), so the variances are not equal.”
How did you make the segments?Average of the 5 items (2 reversed), α = .77, cut at the cumulative % closest to 33.33 and 66.66.
Why did you use only some respondents?The RQ is about the high-concern segment only → Select Cases seg = 1.
The effect is small: does it matter?Significant = real in the population; small = groups overlap, so we combine it with other results before acting.
Is H1 supported?Say clearly: supported, partially supported or not supported, and for which DV.
RQ1: why Cramer’s V and not Phi?Phi is for 2×2 tables; with 3 segments the table is larger, so Cramer’s V (S4).
RQ1: why did you merge categories of occupation / education / language?The Chi² condition failed (too many expected counts < 5); the course says merge cells or stop (S4).
RQ1: the effects are negligible, so is the profile useful?The differences are real in the population (significant) but small: the profile tells us where to focus, not that other people will not buy.
RQ6: why the bottom row for attractiveness?Levene p = .002 → variances not equal → “equal variances not assumed” row: t(184.21) = 2.02, p = .045.
RQ6: self-control p = .054, isn’t that almost significant?No: p > .050, so the groups are equal in the population. We don’t say one is lower.
What would you do as product manager?Repeat the recommendation slide, linked to a number.
